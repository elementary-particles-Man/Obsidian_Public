# Budget and Execution Mechanics in the Fiat System (Practical Guide)

This guide explains, in practical terms, how modern governments operate annual budgets and debt markets under the fiat (non-convertible) monetary system that has prevailed since the 1971 Nixon shock.

## 1. A Common Annual Framework (Cash Operations)

- Opening cash at the start of the fiscal year is set to “0”.
- The first operational step is issuing short-term government securities (T-Bills or equivalents) to obtain spendable cash.
- Over the year, the government redeems short-term bills as revenue arrives and re-issues as needed to bridge gaps.
- At fiscal year-end, all short-term bills are repaid, returning the cash position to “0”.

This framework smooths intra-year cash flows and uses short-term rates/liquidity as macro stabilizers.

## 2. Japan (Example)

- Short-term government securities are effectively redeemed “with taxes as they arrive”.
- In this framing, short-term securities themselves do not move through a conventional primary market (assumption for this guide’s exposition).

### 2.1 Primary Market (New Issues)
- Definition: The Ministry of Finance issues new JGBs, and Primary Dealers (PDs) acquire them via auctions.
- Funding pool: PDs fund purchases using institution-only pools (in Japan, mainly Bank of Japan current account balances).
- Key point: The system is designed so that, as a rule, “no uncovered leftovers” occur at auctions.

### 2.2 Secondary Market (Outstanding Issues)
- Definition: Investors trade outstanding bonds via PDs.
- Japan-specific features:
  - PDs are “principally hold-to-maturity”; they sell when requested, but HtM on balance sheet is the baseline posture.
  - The Bank of Japan functions as a safety valve in the secondary market, so PDs face limited mark-to-market loss concerns.
  - The secondary market is relatively smaller; even rises in ultra-long yields have limited direct implications for sovereign credit.

## 3. United States (Example)

- Short-term securities are issued as Treasury Bills (TBs) and sold in the primary market like other Treasuries.

### 3.1 Primary Market (New Issues)
- Definition: The U.S. Treasury auctions new securities acquired by PDs.
- Funding pool: PDs combine their own funding, repo, and Federal Reserve reserve balances to bid.
- Key point: The U.S. framework also minimizes auction “failures,” effectively preventing uncovered supply.

### 3.2 Secondary Market (Outstanding Issues)
- U.S.-specific features:
  - PDs acquire new issues with the “intent to profit in secondary,” not to hold to maturity. Treasuries are trading P&L tools.
  - The Federal Reserve is not a standing secondary-market backstop; PDs price expected secondary P&L at the primary stage.
  - The secondary market is highly active; yields across the curve are read directly as sovereign credit signals.

## 4. Primary vs. Secondary (At a Glance)

- Primary market:
  - Participants: Designated Primary Dealers only.
  - Funding: Institution-only pools (Japan: BOJ current accounts; U.S.: reserves/repo, etc.).
  - Purpose: Initial allocation of government funding; systems are built to avoid uncovered supply.

- Secondary market:
  - Participants: Investors (institutional/retail) and PDs.
  - Purpose: Price discovery, portfolio rebalancing, and liquidity for outstanding issues.
  - Japan: HtM posture + BOJ safety valve; yields less tightly linked to “credit” in day-to-day pricing.
  - U.S.: Trading-centric posture; yields function as a thermometer of sovereign credit across the curve.

## 5. Conclusion: What Taxes Do in a Fiat Regime

Under the post-1971 fiat standard, taxes are not the government’s “source of money” per se. Functionally, taxes:

- Remove private purchasing power (cooling inflation/overheating),
- Shape distribution and behavior (social policy/incentives), and
- Anchor currency demand (the obligation to pay taxes in the unit of account).

Japan’s practice of redeeming short-term government securities with tax receipts reflects an operational choice within the institutional design, not a hard funding constraint. Taxes are best understood as macro-stabilization tools—lubricants and thermostats—not as the mechanical “source of funds.”
